## 0. 执行摘要（中文）

**一句话结论：** 你的猜想有一半站得住，另一半需要换个说法。"Seven 能在脑子里直接看到想要的设计" 这件事，认知科学里有真实、可测的对应物——**心理表象（mental imagery）的清晰度和可操作性**，它在人群中是一条从"完全看不见"（aphantasia，约 1–4%）到"和真实所见一样清楚"（hyperphantasia，约 2–3%）的连续谱。**但把它归因于 ISFP，是把一个可测量的认知能力挂在一个不可测量的标签上。** MBTI 的功能栈（Fi–Se–Ni–Te）在实证研究里没有任何支撑，"ISFP = 靠直观画面工作的艺术家" 是社群叙事，不是研究结论。

**研究实际支持的是这几点：**

1. **表象清晰的人确实在"创意类"职业里更常见**（Zeman 等 2020，问卷样本 2,200 人），而且"对象表象"（object imagery：颜色、质感、形状的整体画面）而不是"空间表象"（spatial imagery：结构关系），是视觉艺术家和设计师的典型优势（Blazhenkova & Kozhevnikov 2010）。摄影/设计正是对象表象的主场。
2. **表象和感官敏感度相关**：表象越强的人，对光线、噪音、图案的实际感官反应也越强（Dance 等 2021）。这和你观察到的"审美很敏锐、对不对一眼就知道"是同一件事的两面，不需要用 Se 来解释。
3. **能"看见"不等于能"做出来"，也不是创意的必要条件。** 皮克斯前总裁 Ed Catmull 和《小美人鱼》的动画师 Glen Keane 都完全没有心理表象，仍是顶级视觉创作者；Catmull 调查了 540 名同事，发现表象能力与艺术岗位无强相关。预测原创性的是**操作画面的能力**（旋转、拆分、组合），不是画面本身有多清楚（Palmiero 等 2015, 2016）。所以 Seven 的天赋更可能是 "表象 + 感官敏感 + 长期练出来的选择判断" 三者叠加，而不是单一的"看见"。

**为什么 Seven 说不清自己的过程（这不是缺陷）：**
- 画面是高维的，语言是低维的；把画面翻译成话，本身就在丢信息。
- 实验证据："**边做边说看到什么**"不影响表现；但"**解释为什么**"会改变思考过程、拖慢速度（Fox 等 2011，94 项研究元分析）。对人脸等视觉记忆，逼人用语言描述会**降低**之后的辨认准确度（"言语遮蔽"，Alogna 等 2014 多实验室复现）。
- 审美判断在几十到几百毫秒内完成，"理由"多是事后重建的。

**睡眠和情绪为什么会直接打掉这个能力（一般机制，不是诊断）：**
- 心理表象靠额顶叶控制网络 + 视觉皮层协同，两者都在缺觉时活性下降（Krause 等 2017）；表象强度和视觉工作记忆容量高度相关，缺觉减少的正是这个容量。
- 表象是"情绪放大器"（Wicken 等 2021）：画面越清楚，情绪越强；反过来，情绪负荷高时，侵入性画面会占用同一套"心里的画布"。对表象强的人，这是双向放大的。

**你能怎么帮（不逼说话，尊重"知道自己不要什么"）：**
1. **给东西看，不要要描述。** 用参考图、并排对比、A/B、实物样品让 Seven 做**选择**，而不是让 Seven 用话**生成**。
2. **允许并善用"否定判断"。** "不是这个" 是模板不匹配的信号，比"要这个"更快也更准。把"排除法"当作正式的工作方法，不当作没主见。
3. **问"看到什么"，不问"为什么"。** 前者无害，后者会改变过程。
4. **把外化当作卸载，不当作证明。** 草图、截图、便签只是帮画面不在工作记忆里蒸发，不需要好看，也不需要解释。
5. **在低电量时段不排需要"从零生成画面"的事。** 缺觉/情绪重时，表象是第一个掉线的能力；排选择、执行、整理类任务更合适。

**不确定性（要诚实）：** 我们没有 Seven 的任何测量数据；上述关联都是群体层面的、多数是问卷相关；"ISFP" 不能作为解释变量；表象强的人是否更容易"过早固定"设计方案（design fixation）只是一个合理猜想，没有直接研究。

**给 Seven 看的时候，可以只看这一段 + 第 5 节。** 其余是给你的论证过程和英文文献。

## 1. The hypothesis, decomposed

The owner's claim bundles three things that the literature treats separately. Keeping them apart is the whole point of this memo.

| Component | Testable? | Literature status |
|---|---|---|
| **H1.** Seven has unusually vivid / controllable visual mental imagery, and uses it as the primary working medium for design. | Yes, in principle (imagery vividness and object-vs-spatial imagery are measurable with validated questionnaires and behavioral tasks). | Real, well-studied construct. Population varies continuously from aphantasia to hyperphantasia. |
| **H2.** This imagery is *why* Seven's taste and inspiration are unusual. | Partially. Imagery is associated with creative occupations and with sensory sensitivity, but causality is unproven and imagery is demonstrably not necessary for visual creativity. | Correlational support; strong counter-examples exist. |
| **H3.** This is explained by Seven being ISFP (Fi-dominant, Se-auxiliary). | No. The MBTI function stack has no empirical support, and the S/N dimension does not measure imagery. | Folklore. Using it as an explanatory variable adds nothing to H1 and H2 and subtracts credibility. |

Recommended restatement: *Seven appears to be a strong object-visualizer with high sensory sensitivity and a preference for non-verbal, comparison-based aesthetic judgment. "ISFP" is a convenient community shorthand for that cluster of traits, not its cause.*

## 2. ISFP: what the folklore says vs. what research supports

### 2.1 The folklore

In popular MBTI / Jungian-typology communities, ISFP is described with a "cognitive function stack" of Fi (introverted Feeling, dominant) → Se (extraverted Sensing, auxiliary) → Ni (introverted Intuition, tertiary) → Te (extraverted Thinking, inferior). The stock narrative:

- **Fi**: a strong internal sense of what feels right or wrong, personally held, hard to articulate, expressed through choices rather than arguments. Community texts frequently gloss this as "knows what they don't like before knowing what they like."
- **Se**: acute, present-tense attention to concrete sensory detail — colour, texture, light, sound; "living in the aesthetic moment."
- **Ni (tertiary)**: a background sense of "where this is going," sometimes described as flashes of imagery or vision. Communities disagree about the attitude of the tertiary (Ni vs. Ne for ISFP); this dispute has never been settled because there is no data that could settle it.
- **Te (inferior)**: discomfort with external systematization, explaining, justifying, and procedural logic.

From this, the community conclusion "ISFP = the Artist / Composer / Adventurer" follows. The MBTI Manual's own occupational tables do list ISFP among the types over-represented in arts and crafts occupations, but those are descriptive frequencies from self-selected samples, not evidence about mechanism.

Note the internal tension that the owner's hypothesis exposes: in MBTI folklore, **imagination and mental imagery are usually attributed to N (Intuition), not S (Sensing)**. An "ISFP who works by seeing the design in the mind" is, within the folklore's own terms, either leaning on the tertiary Ni or is not a textbook ISFP. This is not a problem with Seven; it is a sign that the S/N dichotomy is the wrong instrument for the question.

### 2.2 What research actually supports

1. **The MBTI dichotomies are continuous, not bimodal.** Scores on E/I, S/N, T/F, J/P are roughly normally distributed; there is no natural "type" boundary (Bess & Harvey 2002; Pittenger 2005; Stein & Swan 2019).
2. **The four MBTI scales map onto four of the Big Five.** S/N correlates strongly with Openness to Experience, T/F with Agreeableness, E/I with Extraversion, J/P with Conscientiousness (McCrae & Costa 1989). MBTI is roughly a coarse, dichotomized Big Five with Neuroticism removed.
3. **"Type dynamics" — the function stack — has no empirical support.** Reynierse (2009), publishing in the MBTI community's own journal, reviewed the evidence and concluded that the dominant/auxiliary/tertiary/inferior ordering does not predict anything beyond the additive contribution of the individual preferences. Stein & Swan (2019) reach the same conclusion from outside the community: the theory lacks testability, disagrees with known data, and contains internal contradictions.
4. **Test–retest instability.** A substantial fraction of retakers change at least one letter within weeks (Pittenger 2005; Randall, Isaacson & Ciro 2017 systematic review), so a single-label "ISFP" should be read as a self-description Seven identifies with, not a stable measurement.

### 2.3 What survives translation into measurable terms

The ISFP narrative is not worthless; it is a folk description of a real trait cluster. The translation:

| Folklore term | Nearest measurable construct | Relevant evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Se — concrete sensory attunement | **Sensory sensitivity** (e.g., Glasgow Sensory Questionnaire) and **object imagery** ability | Dance, Ward & Simner 2021: imagery strength and sensory sensitivity are positively related, in aphantasics and in the general population. Blazhenkova & Kozhevnikov 2010: object imagery uniquely predicts visual-art specialization. |
| Fi — personal, hard-to-verbalize aesthetic judgment | **Idiosyncratic aesthetic taste** for human-made artifacts; **fast, pre-reflective aesthetic evaluation** | Vessel et al. 2018: taste for artifacts (architecture, design, art) is far more individual than taste for natural scenes and faces. Skov & Nadal 2020; Pelowski et al. 2017: aesthetic evaluation is a fast, bottom-up-plus-top-down process whose reasons are largely reconstructed afterward. |
| "Artist type" | Higher **Openness**, especially the *aesthetic sensitivity* aspect | McCrae & Costa 1989 (S/N ↔ Openness). Zeman et al. 2020 and Milton et al. 2021: hyperphantasics score higher on Openness than both aphantasics and controls. |
| Ni "vision" / Te discomfort | **Visualizer (vs. verbalizer) cognitive style** | Palmiero, Nori & Piccardi 2016: visualizer style predicts originality of visual inventions. Fox, Ericsson & Best 2011: demands to explain change the process (see §4.3). |

Every row of the right-hand column can be measured and has replicated literature. None of the left-hand column can. That asymmetry is the practical reason to drop "ISFP" as an explanation while keeping it as Seven's self-description if Seven finds it useful.

## 3. Cognitive science of visual mental imagery

### 3.1 The spectrum

- **Definition.** Mental imagery is "the quasi-sensory experience of items, typically in their absence." **Aphantasia** is the absence or marked reduction of *voluntary* sensory imagery; **hyperphantasia** is imagery whose vividness rivals perception (Monzel et al. 2022; Zeman et al. 2025 definition note). The terms were introduced in Zeman, Dewar & Della Sala (2015).
- **Prevalence.** Extreme aphantasia ≈ 0.7–3.9% depending on cut-off; extreme hyperphantasia ≈ 2.5–3% (Zeman et al. 2020; Dance, Ipser & Simner 2022; Zeman 2024 review). Most people sit in the middle. Vividness is usually measured with the Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire (VVIQ; Marks 1973), object-vs-spatial preference with the Object–Spatial Imagery Questionnaire (OSIQ; Blazhenkova & Kozhevnikov 2009).
- **It is real, not just a self-report style.** Aphantasics show no imagery-based priming in binocular rivalry (Keogh & Pearson 2018), reduced pupil response to imagined brightness, flat skin-conductance response to imagined (but not perceived) frightening scenarios (Wicken, Keogh & Pearson 2021), and impaired object-detail but intact spatial recall when drawing from memory (Bainbridge et al. 2021). Milton et al. (2021) found distinct resting-state connectivity signatures for aphantasia vs. hyperphantasia. Chang et al. (2025, *Current Biology*) could still decode "imagery" content from early visual cortex in aphantasics, but the representation was ipsilateral and did not cross-decode with perception — i.e., something is computed, but it does not produce a picture-like experience.

### 3.2 Mechanism (why "seeing it in the head" is not mystical)

Imagery reuses the perceptual machinery top-down. Frontoparietal control regions drive the visual cortex to reinstate a pattern similar to what perception would produce; the image is weaker and more schematic than perception, and "reality monitoring" relies substantially on signal strength (Pearson 2019; Dijkstra, Bosch & van Gerven 2019; Dijkstra & Fleming 2023). Two consequences matter here:

1. **Imagery is a perceptual template.** Holding an image sets up the visual system so that a matching real stimulus is detected faster and a mismatching one "feels wrong" almost immediately (imagery priming and imagery-as-added-sensory-signal effects; Pearson 2019; Dijkstra & Fleming 2023). This is the non-mystical basis for "I'll know it when I see it" and, more so, for "that is *not* it."
2. **Imagery competes for the same workspace as perception and working memory.** Imagery strength predicts visual working-memory capacity (Keogh & Pearson 2014). Anything that consumes that workspace — sleep loss, emotional arousal, a noisy environment — degrades imagery first.

### 3.3 Object vs. spatial imagery: the distinction that matters for a designer

Blazhenkova & Kozhevnikov (2009, 2010) split "visualizer" into two dissociable abilities:

- **Object imagery**: vivid, high-resolution pictorial images of colour, shape, texture, lighting. Visual artists, photographers, and designers score high; they describe their images as "bright, pictorial, whole."
- **Spatial imagery**: schematic representations of structure, location, and transformation. Scientists, engineers, and architects score high; artists often score *lower* than scientists here.

The two trade off: people strong in one tend to be weaker in the other. This is directly useful: if Seven's strength is object imagery, tasks that require spatial-schematic reasoning (layout systems, information architecture, versioned iterations across many screens) will feel disproportionately expensive even when Seven's aesthetic judgment on each individual frame is excellent. That is a profile, not a deficiency.

### 3.4 Imagery and creative design practice — what the evidence supports and what it does not

**Supports:**
- Hyperphantasia is over-represented in "creative" professions and aphantasia in scientific/mathematical ones (Zeman et al. 2020, n ≈ 2,200 questionnaires; corroborated by Dawes et al. 2020).
- Object imagery ability predicts visual-art specialization beyond general intelligence (Blazhenkova & Kozhevnikov 2010) and artistic creativity in team studies (Blazhenkova & Kozhevnikov 2016).
- Visualizer cognitive style and imagery *transformation* ability predict originality and aesthetic quality of visual inventions and drawings (Palmiero et al. 2015, 2016).
- Expert designers can conduct early conceptual design entirely internally: blindfolded expert architects produced designs judged equal in quality to their sketched designs (Bilda, Gero & Purcell 2006). Externalization is helpful but not required for those with strong internal representations.
- In photography specifically, "visualization" — seeing the finished print before exposure — is a canonical craft skill, not an exotic one (Adams 1981, *The Negative*). Seven's process sits in a documented professional tradition.

**Does not support:**
- **Imagery is not necessary for visual creativity.** Ed Catmull (co-founder of Pixar) and Glen Keane (animator of Ariel, *The Little Mermaid*) both have aphantasia. Catmull surveyed 540 former colleagues and found no strong relationship between imagery ability and artistic vs. technical roles (BBC 2019). Zeman (2024) draws the same conclusion from the research literature: "sensory imagery is not a prerequisite for creative imagination."
- **Raw vividness is not what predicts originality.** In Palmiero et al. (2015), *transformation* ability and visualizer *style* predicted originality; vividness per se correlated with practicality, not originality. The useful skill is manipulating the image, not merely having it.
- **Imagery does not guarantee execution.** Imagery ability and drawing skill are largely separate; drawing accuracy depends on perceptual encoding and motor practice (Chamberlain & Wagemans 2016). Catmull makes the same point from the studio floor.

Net: the owner's hypothesis is best supported in the form "Seven's *object* imagery and sensory sensitivity plausibly shape *what Seven selects and rejects*," and weakest in the form "Seven's imagery is the source of the creativity."

## 4. A non-mystical account of "see it, then make it" for an ISFP-leaning photographer/designer

### 4.1 The working model

Proposed pipeline, each step grounded above:

1. **Sensory intake with high gain.** High sensory sensitivity (§2.3, Dance et al. 2021) means light, colour, material, and rhythm register strongly and are richly encoded.
2. **Template formation.** From that intake and from an internal, idiosyncratic aesthetic (Vessel et al. 2018), a candidate object-image forms in the mind's eye — whole, pictorial, with colour and texture already resolved (§3.3).
3. **Fast match/mismatch evaluation.** Real candidates (a frame in the viewfinder, a layout, a swatch) are compared against the template. Mismatch is detected quickly and confidently (§3.2, imagery-as-template). This is the "knows what is *not* it" signal.
4. **Realization by iteration against the template.** Camera settings, edits, layout moves are steered until the external artifact stops producing the mismatch signal. The template may itself update during this loop.
5. **Post-hoc explanation, if demanded.** Reasons are reconstructed after the fact. They are approximate and frequently wrong about the actual causes (Nisbett & Wilson 1977; Skov & Nadal 2020).

Nothing here requires special access to anything. It is perception, working memory, and expertise operating in a slightly unusual configuration.

### 4.2 Strengths this configuration predicts

- **Embodied, whole-image aesthetics.** Decisions arrive as gestalts rather than as checklists; the result tends to be internally coherent because it was matched against a single integrated image.
- **Rapid sensory selection.** Culling shoots, picking materials, rejecting a layout: fast and consistent, because mismatch detection is cheap.
- **Low dependence on external tooling in early concept work.** Like Bilda et al.'s architects, Seven can probably do serious design work without a sketchbook, on a walk, or in the dark.
- **Reference-driven communication works.** Show Seven an image and the match/mismatch judgment is immediate and reliable; this is a communication channel with high bandwidth if the other party uses it.

### 4.3 Failure modes (and why they are structural, not character flaws)

**(a) The process is hard to verbalize — and forcing it is costly.**
- The image is high-dimensional; language is a lossy, serial channel. Translation is genuine work, not evasion.
- Fox, Ericsson & Best (2011), meta-analysis of 94 studies: simply *saying what comes to mind* ("think-aloud") does not change task performance; but instructions to **describe or explain** *do* change the process and consistently slow it. The change is not always for the worse, but the process is no longer the same process.
- For visual memory specifically, being made to describe a face in words *impairs* later recognition of it — verbal overshadowing, confirmed in a multi-lab registered replication (Alogna et al. 2014; Schooler & Engstler-Schooler 1990). Evidence that verbalization also impairs insight problem-solving (Schooler, Ohlsson & Brooks 1993) is more mixed and should be weighted accordingly.
- Practical reading: "describe what you see" is safe; "justify why" is an intervention. Repeatedly demanding justification trains Seven to do the design in the verbal channel, which is the weaker channel.

**(b) Sleep loss and emotional load knock out imagery early.**
- Imagery depends on frontoparietal control plus visual cortex. Acute sleep deprivation reduces task-related activity in exactly those regions (dorsolateral prefrontal, parietal, extrastriate visual cortex) and lowers visual working-memory capacity — the number of items held, not their precision (Krause et al. 2017; Wee et al. 2013; Xie et al. 2019). Since imagery strength and visual WM capacity are tightly coupled (Keogh & Pearson 2014), the predicted effect of a bad night is: fewer or blurrier templates, more effort to hold one steady, less capacity to transform it.
- Imagery is an **emotional amplifier**: aphantasics show a flat physiological response to imagined frightening scenarios while responding normally to perceived ones (Wicken et al. 2021). The mirror image is that strong imagers get *more* emotional punch from their own thoughts. Under emotional load, intrusive or ruminative images occupy the same visual workspace the design template needs (Pearson et al. 2015; Ji et al. 2016). For a strong object-imager, emotional state and design capacity are therefore mechanically coupled, in both directions.
- Practical reading: when Seven says "I can't see it today," that is likely a literal report about a degraded working medium, not a motivational statement. This is a general cognitive-load account; it says nothing about any clinical condition and this memo does not go there.

**(c) The imagery–reality gap.**
- Imagery is weaker and less detailed than perception (Dijkstra & Fleming 2023). A very vivid imager can experience the internal image as "already finished," and the realized artifact may disappoint because it cannot match a template that was never fully specified. Symptoms: perfectionism at the execution stage, abandoning near-finished work, "it's not what I saw."
- Chamberlain & Wagemans (2016) is relevant: execution skill is separate from imagery and improves with deliberate practice on encoding and motor control, not with more imagining.

**(d) Fixation risk (hypothesis, not established).**
- Design fixation — premature commitment to an early concept — is a documented problem for professional designers (Crilly 2015). It is plausible that a vivid, whole, early template raises fixation risk because alternatives have to compete with something that already looks real. No study tests this in hyperphantasic designers specifically; treat as a watch-item.

**(e) Object/spatial trade-off.**
- Tasks that are spatial-schematic rather than pictorial (system-wide consistency, multi-screen information architecture, version management) may be disproportionately tiring (§3.3). Pairing Seven with a spatial-imagery-strong collaborator, or with tooling that handles structure, is a profile-matched fix.

## 5. How the owner can support without forcing verbalization

Principle: **shift the interaction from generation-in-words to selection-among-things.** Every item below saves Seven the cost of translating the image into language and uses the match/mismatch channel that is Seven's strong one.

### 5.1 Communication practices

| Do | Instead of | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Show two or three concrete options (images, swatches, prints, mockups) and ask "which, or neither?" | "Describe what you want." | Recognition is cheaper than generation; mismatch detection is Seven's fastest channel (§3.2). Elimination-by-aspects is a legitimate decision procedure (Tversky 1972; Shafir 1993). |
| Ask "what do you see?" / "what's off?" | "Why do you like this?" / "Explain your reasoning." | Think-aloud is non-reactive; explanation demands alter the process (Fox et al. 2011). |
| Accept "not this" as a complete, useful answer, and iterate. | Treating rejection without a stated reason as indecision. | Reverse preference is the mismatch signal; it is data, not evasiveness. |
| Use reference images, mood boards, physical samples as the shared language. | Written briefs as the primary medium. | Same channel the imagery operates in. |
| Let Seven answer later, or answer by making something. | Requiring a verbal answer in the moment. | Verbal overshadowing risk; the image may be easier to *show* than to *say*. |

### 5.2 Respecting reverse preference ("I know what I don't want")

- Treat it as a method, not a limitation. Convergent design by elimination is standard practice; many expert designers describe their taste primarily as constraints (Crilly 2015 interviews).
- Operationalize it: maintain a lightweight "not this" collection alongside any "this" collection. Over time the negative space defines the positive taste with less effort than trying to articulate the positive directly.
- Do not demand a positive spec as the price of taking a rejection seriously. The positive image often does not exist yet in words, only as a partially resolved template.

### 5.3 Cognitive-load management (general, non-clinical)

- **Externalize to offload, not to prove.** A phone photo, a screenshot, a three-line sketch, a saved reference — these keep the template from evaporating out of working memory (Kirsh 2010; Goldschmidt 1991). They do not need to be legible to anyone else, and Seven should not be asked to explain them.
- **Schedule generation for high-capacity windows.** Template formation (step 2 in §4.1) is the most WM-hungry step. After poor sleep or during emotional load, route work toward selection, execution, and tidying, and away from "make something new from nothing."
- **Reduce competing sensory input during generation.** High sensory sensitivity is a double-edged trait (Dance et al. 2021): the same gain that makes intake rich makes noisy environments costly.
- **Do not interpret "I can't see it" as unwillingness.** See §4.3(b).

### 5.4 If Seven wants to explore this (optional, self-directed)

Only if Seven is curious, not as a test the owner administers: the VVIQ (Marks 1973) and the OSIQ (Blazhenkova & Kozhevnikov 2009) are short, public, non-clinical questionnaires. They would turn "ISFP" into two numbers that actually mean something here: vividness, and object-vs-spatial preference. If Seven is not interested, nothing in §5.1–5.3 depends on it.

### 5.5 What not to do

- Do not use "ISFP" to predict what Seven *should* be good or bad at, or to explain conflicts. The label's predictive validity is close to zero (§2.2).
- Do not present imagery as a gift that explains everything; Catmull and Keane are the standing counter-example, and the framing quietly devalues the practice and judgment Seven has actually built.
- Do not slide from cognitive load into diagnosis. This memo deliberately stays at the level of sleep-and-emotion-as-load because that is what the imagery literature supports.

## 6. Explicit uncertainties

1. **No measurement of Seven exists.** Everything above is a general model fitted to an owner observation. Seven might be a strong object-imager, or might be a mid-range imager with exceptionally trained perceptual judgment; both produce the observed behaviour.
2. **All imagery–occupation links are correlational and mostly self-report.** Zeman et al.'s 2020 sample is self-selected (people who wrote in after press coverage). Direction of causation is unknown.
3. **Sensory sensitivity ↔ imagery** (Dance et al. 2021) is one lab's finding with behavioural corroboration; it has not been extensively replicated.
4. **Hyperphantasia is much less studied than aphantasia.** Claims about very vivid imagers are extrapolated from small groups (n ≈ 25 in Milton et al. 2021).
5. **Fixation risk in vivid imagers** (§4.3d) is my inference, not a finding.
6. **Verbal overshadowing** replicates for face memory; its extension to aesthetic judgment and insight is plausible but less secure.
7. **MBTI**: "ISFP" is treated throughout as Seven's self-description. Nothing in this memo depends on it being "true," and the memo argues it cannot be true or false in a measurable sense.
8. **Mixed pronoun usage in the source material**: this memo uses the name "Seven" throughout and makes no claims about gender.

## 7. Scope boundary

The owner brief mentioned ongoing sleep and emotional support and an earlier clinical label. This memo does not address either. Sleep and emotion appear only where the imagery literature directly addresses them as cognitive-load factors (§4.3b, §5.3). No clinical inference is made or implied, and none should be read into the load-management suggestions.
